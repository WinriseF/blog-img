Why Passkeys May Finally Replace the Password

For decades, the password has survived despite almost everyone knowing its weaknesses. People forget passwords, reuse them across websites, choose simple ones, and sometimes hand them to criminals without realizing it. Companies have responded with password rules, text-message codes, authenticator apps, and security warnings. Yet the basic problem has remained: a password is a secret that a person must somehow send to a service in order to prove who they are.

A different approach is now becoming common. It is called a passkey. In 2026, the FIDO Alliance, an industry group that develops authentication standards, reported that more than five billion passkeys were in use worldwide. Major technology platforms have spent several years adding support for them, and many banks, retailers, software services, and other online businesses now offer passkey sign-in. The change is important because a passkey is not simply a stronger password. It changes the basic design of online authentication.

When a person creates a traditional password, the website must keep enough information to check that password later. Good security systems do not normally store the password itself, but attackers can still steal password databases and try to recover weak passwords. There is another problem outside the database. A fake website can copy the appearance of a real login page and ask a victim to type a password into it. This is phishing, and even a very strong password can be stolen this way.

Passkeys work differently. They use public-key cryptography. When a passkey is created, the user's device produces a pair of related digital keys. One is a public key, which the website can store. The other is a private key, which remains under the user's control. During sign-in, the website sends a challenge. The private key is used to produce a mathematical response, and the website checks that response with the public key. The private key itself does not need to travel across the internet.

This design removes an important target. If criminals break into a company's database and steal public keys, those keys cannot be used to sign in as customers. There is no shared password waiting to be recovered. More importantly, a passkey is connected to the website for which it was created. A passkey for one service cannot simply be entered into a convincing copy of that service. This is why passkeys are described as phishing-resistant.

The experience can also be simpler than the technology sounds. On a phone or laptop, a user may visit a login page and approve the sign-in with a fingerprint, facial recognition, or the device's PIN. The biometric information is not sent to the website. It is normally processed locally by the device, which only confirms that the person was allowed to use the private key. From the user's point of view, the result can feel like unlocking a phone rather than entering a password and then waiting for a second security code.

Convenience matters because security systems often fail when they demand too much from ordinary people. Complicated password rules encourage predictable habits, such as changing one number at the end of an old password. Extra authentication steps improve security in many situations, but they also create more opportunities for confusion and abandoned logins. According to research published by the FIDO Alliance in 2026, nearly half of surveyed consumers said they were likely to abandon a purchase or sign-in when they could not remember their password. Organizations using passkeys also reported benefits such as faster employee logins and fewer password-reset requests.

The difficult part is making passkeys work across the messy collection of devices people actually use. A credential that existed only on one phone would become a serious problem if the phone were lost. Modern passkey systems therefore often allow credentials to be synchronized through a passkey provider. Apple, for example, says passkeys can sync through iCloud Keychain with end-to-end encryption. Other platforms and password managers provide similar forms of synchronization. Cross-device methods can also allow a person to use a passkey from a nearby phone when signing in on another computer.

This convenience introduces a new question: if passwords disappear, what becomes the key to a person's digital life? In many cases, the answer is the account that protects the user's devices and synchronized credentials. That makes device security, account recovery, and passkey-provider security especially important. A technology can remove one class of risk while increasing the importance of another part of the system. Passkeys do not make account security automatic, and companies still need careful recovery procedures for people who lose devices or access to their main accounts.

There is also a transition problem. The internet cannot replace passwords everywhere at once. Some services support passkeys but keep passwords as a backup. This is understandable, because users need ways to recover accounts and older devices may not support newer authentication methods. However, if an attacker can still choose the weaker password route, some of the security benefit is lost. Organizations therefore have to think about the entire sign-in and recovery process, not merely add a passkey button to an existing page.

Even so, the direction is significant. For many years, the security industry tried to improve passwords by asking people to create better secrets and protect them more carefully. Passkeys take a different view. Instead of training billions of people to become better managers of shared secrets, they redesign the system so that the most valuable secret does not have to be shared at all.

That may be the most interesting lesson of the passkey movement. Better security does not always come from making users work harder. Sometimes it comes from changing the technology so that a common human mistake is no longer dangerous in the same way. The password became part of daily life because it was simple to build and easy to understand. Passkeys are becoming practical because modern devices can hide much of their complexity. If the transition continues, future internet users may find it strange that people once had to remember dozens of secret phrases just to prove who they were.
