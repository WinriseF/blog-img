Can AI Data Centers Learn When Not to Use Electricity?

A data center is usually imagined as a building that never sleeps. Its servers answer searches, store photographs, run business software, and increasingly power artificial intelligence. Electricity flows into the facility every hour of the day. For years, the main energy question was how to make these machines more efficient. Now another question is becoming just as important: can some computing work wait until the electricity grid is less busy?

The question matters because artificial intelligence is changing the scale of data centers. According to the International Energy Agency, data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of global electricity consumption. The agency expects that figure to more than double to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, with AI as the largest driver of the increase. Globally, this is still a modest share of all electricity use. Locally, however, the effect can be much larger because data centers tend to gather in a small number of places.

This creates a difficult mismatch. Technology companies can build a data center relatively quickly, but new power lines, substations, transformers, and generating plants often take much longer. The International Energy Agency has warned that grid problems could delay a significant share of planned data center projects. In other words, the challenge is not simply producing enough electricity over an entire year. The electricity must also be available in the right place at the right moment.

One possible answer is demand response. The idea has existed in the electricity industry for years. When demand becomes unusually high, a factory may temporarily reduce production, or a smart thermostat may slightly change the temperature in a home. This lowers pressure on the grid during the most difficult hours. Data centers are now beginning to explore the same principle, but with computing rather than industrial machines or air conditioners.

Google has become one of the clearest examples. In 2025, the company announced agreements with two American utilities that would allow some machine-learning workloads to be reduced or shifted when the local grid was under stress. By March 2026, Google said it had integrated a total of one gigawatt of data-center demand-response capacity into long-term agreements with several U.S. utilities. The basic idea is simple: not every computing task needs to happen immediately.

Consider the difference between two AI jobs. A person asking an AI assistant a question expects an answer within seconds. Delaying that request for an hour would make the service almost useless. Training part of a large model, processing a batch of stored data, or performing some internal analysis may be different. Certain jobs can sometimes be moved to another hour without users noticing. If thousands of computers reduce such flexible work during a period of extreme electricity demand, the data center can behave less like a fixed load and more like an active participant in the power system.

This flexibility becomes especially useful as grids depend more heavily on wind and solar power. Electricity from these sources changes with weather and time of day. Solar generation can be abundant around midday and disappear after sunset. Wind output can rise or fall over several hours. Traditionally, electricity systems have responded by adjusting supply to match demand. Flexible computing introduces another possibility: some demand can move to match supply.

There are limits, of course. A data center cannot simply shut down whenever electricity becomes expensive. Online services need high reliability, and many AI workloads are time-sensitive. Moving a computing job may also create technical problems. Servers, networks, storage systems, and software schedules are closely connected. A task delayed in one place may slow another task somewhere else. Companies therefore need software that can identify which work is flexible and move it without damaging performance.

The problem becomes even more complex because modern AI systems can change their power use rapidly. The International Energy Agency says AI training and model use can produce large and fast swings in electricity demand. At the same time, individual AI server racks are becoming far more power-dense. This means future data centers may need batteries and smarter control systems not only to reduce total consumption, but also to smooth sudden changes in demand.

Flexibility is also not a substitute for building more energy infrastructure. Global data-center electricity use is growing too quickly for scheduling alone to solve the problem. New renewable generation, transmission lines, storage, natural-gas plants, and nuclear projects are all being considered or built to support rising demand. The International Energy Agency expects renewables to provide nearly half of the additional electricity needed by data centers through 2030, while natural gas and other sources will also play major roles.

What demand response can change is how efficiently that infrastructure is used. Electricity networks are often designed for the highest levels of demand, even if those peaks occur only during limited periods. If a large customer can temporarily reduce consumption at those moments, a utility may be able to connect more customers without immediately building enough infrastructure to serve everyone at maximum power at exactly the same time.

This idea is spreading beyond the data center itself. In June 2026, Google and energy technology company Voltus announced an agreement designed to unlock up to 100 megawatts of capacity in the PJM electricity region in the United States. Instead of reducing only Google's own computing demand, the program is intended to coordinate flexible resources such as batteries and smart thermostats in homes and businesses. Participants can reduce demand when the grid needs help, creating additional room for electricity growth elsewhere.

The broader lesson is that the relationship between computing and energy is becoming more interactive. For most of the internet era, software developers could treat electricity as an invisible resource. A program ran when someone wanted it to run, and the power system was expected to provide whatever energy was necessary. AI is making that assumption harder to maintain because the physical scale of computing is becoming too large to ignore.

Future software may therefore need to understand more than processors, memory, and networks. For some workloads, it may also need to understand time, location, electricity prices, and grid conditions. The most efficient AI system may not simply be the one that completes every task as soon as possible. It may be the one that knows which tasks truly cannot wait, and which ones can pause for a better moment.
