Why Data Centers Are Learning to Live Without Water

A modern data center looks more like a warehouse than a power plant, yet it has one problem that both buildings share: heat. Thousands of servers work day and night, turning electricity into computation and, inevitably, heat. If that heat is not removed, electronic equipment becomes less reliable or shuts down. For years, many large data centers have used water as part of the cooling process. As artificial intelligence expands, however, the industry is being forced to ask a difficult question: how much water should computing consume?

The question matters because AI is changing the scale of data-center infrastructure. The International Energy Agency reported that electricity demand from data centers rose by 17 percent in 2025, while demand from AI-focused facilities increased even faster. It expects total data-center electricity use to double by 2030. More computing power means more heat, and more heat means cooling systems must work harder. In an efficient hyperscale data center, cooling may account for a relatively small part of electricity use, but in less efficient facilities it can consume more than 30 percent.

Water became attractive because evaporation is an effective way to remove heat. In some cooling systems, warm water is exposed to moving air. A small part of it evaporates, carrying heat away and cooling the rest. This can use less electricity than relying entirely on mechanical refrigeration, especially in hot weather. The engineering is sensible, but the environmental result depends strongly on location. Water that seems abundant in one region may be a scarce public resource in another.

That conflict has already become visible. In 2024, an environmental court in Chile required Google to revise part of the environmental assessment for a planned data center in Santiago. The court wanted the project to consider how climate change could affect water availability. The concern was not theoretical. Central Chile had experienced a long drought, and local communities were already worried about pressure on groundwater. Similar debates have appeared in other dry regions where large data centers compete indirectly with homes, farms, and industry for limited supplies.

The rapid growth of AI makes these local arguments part of a global engineering problem. Reuters reported in 2025 that a mid-sized data center can use about 1.4 million liters of water per day, although actual consumption varies greatly with design, climate, workload, and cooling method. This is why a simple statement such as “AI uses this much water” can be misleading. Some facilities consume substantial freshwater directly. Others use little or no water on site but still depend on electricity generation that may itself require water.

One possible answer is to stop consuming water during cooling. Microsoft has been moving toward closed-loop systems in which liquid circulates through a sealed network. The liquid collects heat from computing equipment, releases that heat elsewhere, and then returns to repeat the process. Because the same liquid is reused, the system can greatly reduce the need to draw fresh water for normal cooling operations. Other companies are developing similar approaches as demand for dense AI hardware increases.

Liquid cooling is becoming more important for another reason. Traditional data centers often cool an entire room with cold air. That works when each server rack produces a moderate amount of heat. AI accelerators, however, can concentrate enormous computing power into a small space. Moving enough cold air through such dense equipment becomes difficult. A liquid can carry heat more efficiently than air, so engineers increasingly bring cooling fluid closer to the chips themselves.

There are several ways to do this. In direct-to-chip cooling, metal plates containing flowing liquid sit against the hottest processors. Another approach is immersion cooling, in which computer equipment is placed in a special electrically nonconductive fluid. Microsoft has even experimented with a system where this fluid boils at a low temperature. The change from liquid to vapor absorbs heat, and the vapor then cools, becomes liquid again, and falls back into the tank. The cycle happens inside a sealed environment.

An even more unusual experiment took the idea outside the building entirely. Microsoft’s Project Natick placed a sealed data-center module on the seafloor near Scotland’s Orkney Islands. The module contained 864 servers and operated underwater for two years. Seawater carried away heat through a heat-exchange system, so the facility did not consume freshwater for cooling. When researchers recovered the module, they found that its servers had failed at only one-eighth the rate of a comparable group on land.

The researchers believed two conditions may have helped. The underwater module was filled with dry nitrogen rather than normal air, reducing corrosion caused by oxygen. It was also sealed away from people, so technicians could not accidentally disturb components while performing maintenance. Project Natick did not prove that future data centers should all be placed in the ocean, but it demonstrated something broader: many assumptions about how servers must be cooled and maintained can be redesigned.

Every solution, however, creates trade-offs. A cooling system that saves water may require more electricity. Advanced liquid systems can cost more to build and may require new server designs, pumps, pipes, and maintenance skills. Older data centers cannot always be converted easily. There is also a difference between direct and indirect water use. A facility may proudly report that it uses almost no water on site while buying electricity from power plants that consume large quantities of water elsewhere.

This is why the environmental debate is gradually moving beyond a single measurement. The important question is not simply how many liters of water a data center uses. Engineers and policy makers need to consider water stress in the local region, the source of electricity, the efficiency of the cooling system, the amount of useful computing produced, and the environmental cost of building the equipment itself. A liter of water used in a rainy coastal region does not have the same social impact as a liter taken from a drought-prone area.

The pressure to solve these problems will probably increase because AI demand continues to grow even as individual computers become more efficient. Better chips can reduce the energy needed for one task, but cheaper and faster computing often encourages people to perform many more tasks. The result is a familiar pattern in technology: efficiency improves, yet total resource use can still rise.

Data centers therefore offer a useful lesson about the physical side of the digital world. An AI answer may appear instantly on a screen, but producing it depends on processors, electricity, buildings, cooling equipment, power grids, and sometimes millions of liters of water. The cloud has never been weightless. As computing becomes a larger part of modern life, the challenge is not merely to make machines more intelligent. It is to design the physical systems behind them so that intelligence can grow without placing unnecessary pressure on the resources around us.
