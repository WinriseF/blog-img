Why AI Data Centers Are Turning to Liquid Cooling

Walk into a traditional data center and one of the first things you notice is the air. Powerful fans push cold air through rows of servers, while other equipment removes the hot air that comes out. For many years, this was the normal way to keep computers within a safe temperature range. But as artificial intelligence demands more computing power, the old approach is reaching practical limits.

The reason is simple: computers turn electricity into heat. A chip may perform billions of operations every second, but the electrical energy it uses does not disappear. In the end, nearly all of it becomes heat that must be moved away. The U.S. Department of Energy describes this as a basic challenge of data-center design: all the electrical energy entering a data center must eventually be rejected to the environment.

That challenge becomes harder when more powerful chips are packed into a smaller space. Modern artificial intelligence systems often use large groups of graphics processing units, or GPUs. These chips are designed to perform many calculations in parallel, which makes them useful for training and running large AI models. But high performance also means high heat density.

Nvidia’s GB200 NVL72 system shows how far this trend has gone. A single rack connects 72 Blackwell GPUs with 36 Grace central processing units. Instead of relying only on traditional air cooling, the rack uses liquid cooling as part of its design. This is not simply an optional feature added for comfort. It helps make such dense computing possible.

Liquid works well because it can carry heat away from a hot surface much more effectively than air. In a direct-to-chip system, pipes bring coolant close to the processors. A metal cold plate sits on a hot chip, absorbs its heat, and transfers that heat into the moving liquid. The warmed liquid then travels elsewhere in the cooling system, where the heat can be removed.

This changes the problem in an important way. With air cooling, engineers must move very large amounts of air through servers and rooms. That requires fans, air handlers, and carefully controlled paths for hot and cold air. As racks become more powerful, the airflow needed can become difficult to provide. Liquid cooling allows engineers to collect much of the heat close to where it is produced.

The idea itself is not new. Supercomputers and high-performance computing centers have used forms of liquid cooling for years. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory in Colorado built a high-performance computing facility that sends warm water directly to server racks. Its design can operate without a conventional compressor-based chiller and can also recover some waste heat for use elsewhere in the building. What is changing now is the scale of commercial demand. AI is pushing techniques once associated mainly with special scientific machines into mainstream data-center planning.

There is more than one form of liquid cooling. Direct-to-chip cooling usually keeps ordinary server electronics dry while liquid passes through sealed cold plates. Another method, immersion cooling, places hardware in a special electrically non-conductive fluid. Because the fluid can touch the equipment without causing a short circuit, it can remove heat from many components at once.

Each design has advantages, but none makes heat disappear. A liquid-cooled data center still needs a way to release the collected heat. Depending on the facility and climate, that may involve cooling towers, dry coolers, chillers, or other equipment. Some systems can use warmer coolant, which may reduce the need for energy-intensive refrigeration. In suitable locations, waste heat can even be reused to warm nearby buildings or support industrial processes.

Liquid cooling also brings new engineering problems. Pipes, pumps, seals, and heat exchangers must operate reliably around expensive computing equipment. Operators need procedures for maintenance and leak detection. Coolant quality must be controlled, and different materials must remain compatible over years of operation. Some direct-to-chip systems still need fans because memory, storage, power components, and networking equipment also produce heat.

Water use is another issue that can be misunderstood. Liquid cooling does not automatically mean a data center consumes more water, and it does not automatically mean it consumes less. A closed loop can circulate the same coolant repeatedly, but the larger facility may still use water when it rejects heat through evaporation. Other designs can reduce or avoid evaporative water use, sometimes with different energy or cost trade-offs. The result depends on the entire cooling system, not simply on whether liquid reaches the chips.

This is why researchers are trying to improve cooling at several levels at once. The U.S. Department of Energy’s COOLERCHIPS program, for example, supports technologies designed to cool very dense computing systems while sharply reducing the energy spent on cooling. The goal is not just to build a better pipe or pump. It is to treat chips, servers, racks, buildings, and heat-rejection equipment as parts of one thermal system.

The shift also affects the economics of the technology industry. A company building an AI data center no longer thinks only about how many GPUs it can buy. It must consider electrical connections, cooling capacity, water availability, mechanical equipment, floor space, and the local climate. The physical infrastructure around the chips can determine how much computing power a building can actually support.

For ordinary users, this may seem far removed from asking a chatbot a question or generating an image. Yet every digital request eventually reaches physical machines. Those machines consume electricity, produce heat, and depend on real buildings and engineering systems. As AI becomes more powerful, the hidden physical side of computing becomes harder to ignore.

Liquid cooling is therefore not just a story about keeping chips cold. It reflects a larger change in computing. For decades, progress often meant making individual components smaller and faster. Now performance increasingly depends on how thousands of powerful components can work together inside the limits of energy, heat, space, and infrastructure. The next advances in AI may depend as much on moving heat efficiently as on performing calculations quickly.
