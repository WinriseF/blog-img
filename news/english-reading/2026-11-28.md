Why Is a Fragile Crystal Challenging the Silicon Solar Panel?

If you have ever looked down on a city from a train, you have probably seen them: dark blue rectangles tilted toward the sun on rooftops and fields. They look settled, like a technology that has finished evolving. For most people, a solar panel simply means silicon, and silicon means the story is over. According to the International Energy Agency, crystalline silicon has accounted for more than 95 percent of the solar photovoltaic market for years. It is abundant, it can be made at enormous scale, and a good panel is expected to sit outdoors for 25 years or more while losing only a small fraction of output. Against that record, any challenger must answer a hard question: why risk changing what already works?

The answer starts with light itself. Sunlight is not one color but a spread of energies, from blue photons that carry a lot of energy to red and infrared photons that carry less. A solar cell is built around a threshold, often called the bandgap, the minimum energy a photon needs to knock an electron loose and create current. Silicon has one fixed threshold. Photons with too little energy pass straight through and are wasted. Photons with far too much energy do create current, but the extra energy is lost as heat. A single material therefore throws away part of the sunlight at both ends. Physicists call the best a single threshold can do under ordinary sunlight the Shockley-Queisser limit, about 33.7 percent efficiency. Real silicon faces an even lower practical ceiling because of losses inside the material. Silicon did not fail. It ran into physics.

Perovskite came from a different direction. The name comes from a mineral crystal structure, a pattern chemists write as ABX3, where atoms can be swapped into the three positions to tune how the material absorbs light. The solar version is usually a lead halide crystal that absorbs sunlight so strongly that a layer a few hundred nanometers thick can do work that needs a much thicker slice of silicon. It can also be formed at low temperatures, which in principle makes it cheaper to manufacture. When Akihiro Kojima and colleagues reported the first perovskite solar cell in the Journal of the American Chemical Society in 2009, it converted only 3.8 percent of sunlight into electricity and barely survived its own test. What kept researchers going was not the starting number but the speed that followed. Few materials have climbed so fast.

The key idea was not to replace silicon outright but to stack the two materials so each handles the light it handles best. In a perovskite-silicon tandem, sunlight first meets the perovskite top cell, tuned to capture higher-energy blue and green light at a higher voltage. The lower-energy red and infrared light that perovskite lets through then reaches the silicon cell underneath. Instead of forcing one threshold to compromise across the whole spectrum, the tandem uses two thresholds and wastes less at both ends. That is why tandems are not bound by the single-junction limit in the same way. LONGi, the Chinese manufacturer that holds the leading records, puts the theoretical ceiling for this design at about 43 percent, well above what silicon alone can reach.

Records alone do not make a product, but they show the physics is no longer hypothetical. LONGi reported a two-terminal silicon-perovskite tandem cell at 34.85 percent efficiency, certified by the U.S. National Renewable Energy Laboratory. It later reported 35.2 percent, included in the 68th edition of the Solar Cell Efficiency Tables compiled by the team led by Martin Green at the University of New South Wales, the field's independent record book. On July 14, 2026, LONGi announced 35.5 percent, certified by the European Solar Test Installation, part of the European Commission's Joint Research Centre. Those cells are small, around one square centimeter. More telling are the larger results: 34.3 percent on a 261-square-centimeter cell, close to a full wafer, and tandem modules at 31.4 percent and 29.4 percent, also independently certified. The gap between cell and module is normal, because wiring, glass, and matching every cell in a panel all cost efficiency. What matters is that it is now measured on real wafers and modules.

Commercial panels have also begun to leave the factory, at pilot scale. On September 5, 2024, Oxford PV, spun out of the University of Oxford, announced the first commercial shipment of perovskite-on-silicon tandem panels to a customer in the United States for a utility-scale installation. The 72-cell panels had a module efficiency of 24.5 percent, and Oxford PV said they could produce up to 20 percent more energy than a standard silicon panel from the same area. The cells were made on a pilot line in Brandenburg an der Havel, Germany, after development the company says began in 2014. A 24.5 percent shipped module sounds modest beside a 35.5 percent laboratory cell, but for a buyer the comparison is different: it is already above what ordinary silicon modules deliver, and every extra point means more electricity from the same roof and field. Where land is expensive or roof space is limited, that changes project economics.

If perovskite were better in every way, the transition would be finished. It is not, and the reason is in the title: the crystal is fragile in ways silicon is not. Silicon is a hard solid whose atoms stay put. Perovskite is a softer ionic crystal, closer to a salt, in which some ions move under heat, light, or voltage. Water vapor can enter at grain boundaries and break the bonds that hold the absorber together. Heat can drive volatile components out. Light and oxygen can create defects that trap charge. This does not mean a panel falls apart on the first rainy day. Modern devices use mixed compositions, protective layers, and glass-glass encapsulation, and laboratory cells now survive thousands of hours in damp-heat tests. But a solar project is financed on 25 to 30 years outdoors. Accelerated tests are a proxy, not a substitute for decades of field data, and lenders know the difference. Until tandems accumulate long outdoor records, they will remain promising but not yet bankable like silicon.

Two further trade-offs remain. The first is lead. The best perovskites still contain lead, in small amounts per panel but enough to require careful factory handling, clear recycling rules, and confidence that damaged modules will not become an environmental problem. Tin alternatives avoid lead but have been less stable and less efficient, so the field is trying to contain and replace lead without losing performance. The second is uniformity. A tandem works well only if perovskite coats a textured silicon wafer evenly, without pinholes, and if top and bottom cells produce matching current, because the weaker cell limits the pair. Doing that once is a scientific achievement. Doing it millions of times, at speed, with high yield, is an industrial one. That is why large manufacturers are building pilot lines rather than converting gigawatt factories at once.

What has changed is the ceiling. For half a century, solar progress meant squeezing silicon closer to a limit it could not cross. Perovskite has shown, with certified cells above 35 percent and tandem modules already shipped, that stacking a tunable crystal on silicon can move that ceiling in practice. What has not changed is the burden of proof. Silicon earned its dominance not by winning a record but by surviving rooftops and deserts for decades at a price the world could afford. The fragile crystal has won the argument about efficiency. It still has to win the slower argument about time.
