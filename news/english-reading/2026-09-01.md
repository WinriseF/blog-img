Why AI Is Becoming an Energy Story

When people use an artificial intelligence chatbot, the experience can feel almost weightless. A question is typed into a small box, and an answer appears a few seconds later. Yet behind that simple exchange is a physical system of servers, cooling equipment, power lines and electricity plants. As artificial intelligence becomes part of search, programming, office work and entertainment, this hidden infrastructure is becoming much harder to ignore.

The International Energy Agency, or IEA, estimates that data centers consumed about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of global electricity use. That is still a small share of the world's total. The more striking fact is the speed of growth. Data-center electricity consumption has been rising much faster than electricity demand as a whole, and AI is now one of the main forces behind that increase.

AI requires unusual amounts of computing power because modern models perform enormous numbers of calculations. Training a large model can involve thousands of specialized chips working together for weeks or months. Once the model has been trained, serving users also requires electricity. This second stage, often called inference, happens every time a model answers a question, creates an image, writes code or performs another task.

A single simple text request is not necessarily an energy disaster. In fact, recent hardware and software improvements have sharply reduced the electricity needed for many basic AI tasks. The IEA reported in 2026 that energy use per AI task had fallen by at least an order of magnitude each year in recent years. A simple text query can now use very little electricity compared with many everyday activities.

But efficiency does not automatically reduce total energy use. When a service becomes cheaper and easier to provide, people often use much more of it. This effect has appeared in transportation, lighting and computing before. AI may follow the same pattern. More efficient models make it practical to put AI into more products, which creates more requests and encourages developers to build applications that would previously have been too expensive.

The type of AI task is also changing. Generating a short paragraph is very different from creating a video or operating an AI agent that repeatedly plans, searches, reads files and calls other software. According to the IEA, reasoning, video generation and agent-like tasks can require hundreds or even thousands of times more energy per request than simple text generation. So the future energy cost of AI depends not only on how many people use it, but also on what they ask it to do.

This helps explain why data centers are becoming an important issue for electricity companies. The IEA projects that global data-center electricity consumption could reach about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, more than twice the 2024 level and slightly more than Japan's present annual electricity consumption. AI is expected to be the largest driver of this growth.

The global number, however, can hide the most difficult part of the problem. Data centers are not evenly spread around the world. They are often built in clusters where companies can find fast network connections, suitable land and reliable electricity. A large AI-focused facility may demand more than 100 megawatts of power. Several large facilities in the same region can therefore place significant pressure on the local grid even if data centers remain a modest part of worldwide electricity use.

Electricity infrastructure also develops at a different speed from software. A technology company can design and build a data center in a few years. New transmission lines, transformers and power stations may take much longer because they require equipment, permits, construction and agreement from local communities. The IEA has warned that grid limitations could delay a meaningful share of planned data-center projects.

This mismatch is changing the relationship between technology companies and the energy industry. Large technology firms have become major buyers of renewable electricity, but wind and solar power alone cannot always provide electricity exactly when a data center needs it. Companies are therefore exploring a wider mix of energy sources. Natural gas remains important in the near term, while technology companies are also supporting nuclear power, advanced geothermal projects and new energy-storage systems.

The environmental picture is consequently more complicated than saying that AI is either clean or dirty. The emissions connected to an AI service depend partly on where its data center is located and how the local electricity is produced. A highly efficient server powered by a carbon-intensive grid can still have a significant footprint. At the same time, new demand from data centers can help finance renewable, nuclear and other low-carbon projects that might otherwise develop more slowly.

There is another side to the relationship. AI does not only consume electricity; it may also help the energy system use electricity more effectively. Machine-learning systems can improve weather forecasts for wind and solar generation, help operators monitor transmission networks, predict equipment failures and search for new battery materials. The IEA estimates that proven AI applications could reduce energy costs in some energy-intensive industries. The technology that creates new pressure on the power system may therefore also become one of the tools used to improve it.

For ordinary users, this means that the environmental question is more useful when it is asked carefully. Counting the energy of one chatbot question may attract attention, but it says little about the larger system. The more important questions concern scale, efficiency, the growth of demanding applications, the location of data centers and the sources of electricity that supply them.

For developers, the lesson is also changing. Computing resources once felt almost abstract because cloud services made it easy to request more processing power without thinking about the machines underneath. AI is bringing the physical limits of computing back into view. Chips need electricity. Dense servers need cooling. Data centers need grid connections. Grids need generation, storage and transmission equipment.

The development of AI is therefore no longer only a story about better algorithms and larger models. It is becoming a story about infrastructure. The next generation of AI systems will be shaped not just by what engineers can make computers do, but by how efficiently those systems operate and how quickly the physical world can supply the energy they require.
