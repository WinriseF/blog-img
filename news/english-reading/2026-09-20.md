Why the AI Boom Is Becoming an Electricity Story

A request to an AI assistant feels almost weightless. You type a question, wait a few seconds, and words appear on a screen. Yet behind that brief exchange is a physical chain of machines: chips running calculations, memory feeding them data, cooling systems removing heat, and power equipment keeping the whole system stable. As artificial intelligence spreads from chatbots into search, coding, video generation, scientific tools, and software agents, that hidden physical layer is becoming one of the technology industry's biggest constraints.

The scale is already visible. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres used about 485 terawatt-hours of electricity worldwide in 2025. That was around one and a half percent of global electricity demand. The same agency says electricity use by data centres grew 17 percent in a single year, while consumption at AI-focused facilities rose even faster. Its central projection now sees global data-centre electricity use reaching about 950 terawatt-hours by 2030, roughly twice the 2025 level.

At first, those numbers can sound less dramatic than the headlines. Three percent of global electricity demand in 2030 would still leave most electricity going to homes, factories, transport, and other industries. The real difficulty is geography. Data centres are not spread evenly across the world. They are often built in clusters near fibre networks, customers, land, and reliable power. A large AI facility can therefore place factory-sized demand on one local grid, even if data centres remain a modest part of global electricity use.

That is why the AI energy problem is often a grid problem rather than a simple question of whether the world can produce enough electricity in total. A new data centre can be planned and built in a few years. New transmission lines, substations, transformers, or power plants may take much longer. The IEA has warned that shortages of equipment such as transformers and gas turbines, together with slow grid connections and permitting, are becoming bottlenecks for new projects. The challenge is not only generating more power, but delivering it to the exact place and time where large computing loads appear.

The United States shows how quickly this change can become important. A report from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, released by the US Department of Energy, estimated that data centres used about 4.4 percent of US electricity in 2023. Depending on how fast computing demand grows, that share could rise to between 6.7 and 12 percent by 2028. The range is wide because the future depends on several moving parts: how many AI systems are used, how efficient chips and software become, what kinds of tasks people ask AI to perform, and how quickly new infrastructure can be built.

Efficiency makes the story more complicated. Modern chips can perform far more work per unit of energy than earlier hardware, and AI companies are constantly improving software so models need fewer calculations for some tasks. The IEA says the energy required for many AI tasks is falling rapidly. But lower energy per task does not guarantee lower total energy use. If AI becomes cheaper and easier to use, people may use it much more often. And the nature of AI work is changing. Simple text generation is relatively light compared with demanding tasks such as video generation, long reasoning processes, or autonomous agents that perform many steps before producing an answer. According to the IEA, some of these advanced uses can require hundreds or even thousands of times more energy per task than simple text generation.

This creates a familiar pattern in technology. A system becomes more efficient, which lowers the cost of using it, and demand then expands. The result may be that total resource use still rises. That does not mean efficiency is useless. In fact, it may be one of the most powerful tools for limiting future electricity demand. The IEA's high-efficiency scenario shows substantially lower data-centre electricity use than its main projection. Better chips, smaller models, smarter scheduling, and more efficient cooling could all reduce pressure on the grid. The point is simply that efficiency and demand must be considered together.

The search for electricity is also changing energy investment. Renewable power is expected to provide a large share of the additional supply needed by data centres, partly because wind and solar projects can often be built faster than large conventional power stations. Natural gas remains important in several markets because it can provide steady, controllable power. Technology companies are also showing growing interest in nuclear and geothermal energy, which can produce electricity for long periods without depending on weather. No single source solves the problem everywhere, because local grids, prices, regulations, and natural resources differ.

There is another reason energy matters so much to AI companies: reliability. A data centre cannot simply lose power every time the grid becomes stressed. Servers are designed to run continuously, and interruptions can damage services used by millions of people. This need for dependable electricity can make data centres difficult customers for grids, but it can also encourage new ideas. Some operators are exploring ways to shift computing jobs to different times or locations, allowing less urgent work to run when electricity is more available. In theory, computing is more flexible than a steel mill or a hospital. A training job might be delayed for an hour or moved to another region. Turning that technical possibility into a normal business practice, however, requires software, contracts, and electricity markets that can coordinate the change.

The environmental impact is therefore not determined by electricity demand alone. It also depends on what generates the electricity. Two data centres using the same amount of power can have very different carbon footprints if one is supplied mainly by low-carbon sources and the other depends heavily on fossil fuels. The IEA expects renewables to meet much of the growth in global data-centre demand, but it also expects natural gas and, in some regions, coal to remain part of the mix in the near term. Rapid growth can make the clean-energy transition harder if new computing demand arrives faster than low-carbon supply.

For ordinary users, this may seem far removed from the experience of opening an AI app. Yet electricity constraints can influence where data centres are built, how much cloud computing costs, which AI services companies can afford to provide, and how quickly new models can be deployed. For governments and utilities, the issue connects technology policy with industrial planning, energy security, and local infrastructure.

The most useful way to think about AI, then, may be to stop seeing it as purely digital. Every model runs somewhere. Every calculation produces heat. Every server depends on wires, transformers, cooling equipment, and power stations. The intelligence may appear on a screen, but the system that produces it is physical. As AI grows, some of its biggest limits may come not from algorithms, but from how quickly the real world can build the energy infrastructure that those algorithms require.
