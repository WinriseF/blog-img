Why Passkeys May Finally Replace the Password

For decades, the password has been one of the most familiar parts of digital life. It is also one of the most troublesome. People forget passwords, reuse them across different websites, choose simple ones, or store them in unsafe places. Companies spend large amounts of money helping users reset them and defending stolen credentials. Attackers, meanwhile, have built an entire industry around phishing pages, leaked password databases, and automated login attempts.

A newer sign-in method called the passkey tries to remove the basic weakness behind all of these problems: the shared secret. With a password, both the user and the online service depend on the same secret text. If that text is stolen, copied, guessed, or entered into a fake website, an attacker may be able to use it. A passkey works differently. Instead of asking the user to remember a secret, it uses a pair of cryptographic keys.

One part of the pair, called the public key, is stored by the website or app. The other part, the private key, remains under the user's control, usually on a phone, computer, password manager, or hardware security key. When the user signs in, the service sends a challenge. The private key proves that it is present by signing that challenge, but the private key itself is never sent to the service.

For ordinary users, this complicated process looks simple. A person may only need to unlock a phone with a fingerprint, face scan, or device PIN. The biometric information does not travel to the website. It stays on the device, which only confirms that the person has successfully unlocked the credential. In other words, the fingerprint is not the passkey. It is one way of gaining permission to use the passkey.

This design also changes what happens during phishing. A password can be typed into almost any text box, including one created by an attacker. A passkey is tied to the real website or application for which it was created. If a criminal builds a convincing copy of a bank's login page on a different domain, the browser or operating system will not normally use the bank's passkey there. This makes passkeys resistant to a large class of phishing attacks.

The technology is not limited to one company. Passkeys are based on standards developed through the FIDO Alliance and the World Wide Web Consortium. Major operating systems and browsers support them, and large technology companies have been pushing them into ordinary consumer accounts. Google began offering passkeys for personal Google Accounts in 2023 and later made them the default sign-in option when possible. Microsoft went further in 2025 by making new Microsoft accounts passwordless by default and giving passwordless methods a more central place in its sign-in experience. Apple supports passkeys through iCloud Keychain and allows them to sync across a user's Apple devices.

The reason companies are interested is not only security. Passwords create friction. A customer who cannot remember a password may abandon a purchase. An employee who gets locked out may need help from an IT department. One-time codes sent by text message also add cost and delay. Microsoft has reported that passkey sign-ins in its own systems are much more successful and faster than traditional password-based flows. The FIDO Alliance has collected similar reports from companies that say passkeys reduce failed logins and support requests.

Yet replacing passwords is harder than inventing a better login method. Passwords have survived for so long partly because they are universal. A person can remember one and type it on almost any device. Passkeys must offer similar convenience without losing their security advantages.

Synchronization is one answer. A passkey created on a phone can be stored by a credential provider and made available on the user's other devices. Apple's iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager, and third-party password managers can provide this kind of service. If a person tries to sign in on a device that does not already contain the passkey, cross-device authentication can also allow a nearby phone to approve the sign-in, often after scanning a QR code.

This convenience introduces a new question: what happens when someone changes ecosystems or credential managers? A user moving from one platform to another does not want to rebuild every account from the beginning. The FIDO Alliance has therefore been developing credential exchange specifications designed to support secure transfer between credential providers. The problem shows that passwordless authentication is not just a cryptography project. It is also a problem of portability, recovery, and user experience.

Account recovery remains especially important. If a service offers a strong passkey login but allows a weak recovery process based on easily stolen information, attackers may simply target the recovery process instead. Services must decide how users can regain access after losing devices without creating an easier route for criminals. Some organizations may prefer passkeys that sync across devices, while high-security environments may use device-bound passkeys stored on dedicated hardware.

There is also a human problem. Many users still do not clearly understand what a passkey is. A button that says "Sign in with a passkey" may sound like another kind of password, even though the technology is very different. People may not know where their passkeys are stored, which device can use them, or what will happen if they replace a phone. Good design therefore matters almost as much as strong cryptography.

The likely transition will be gradual. For some time, many services will support passwords, passkeys, and other login methods at the same time. That mixed period can be confusing, and older fallback methods may remain attractive targets for attackers. But the direction is becoming clearer. Authentication is moving away from something people must remember and toward something their devices can prove securely.

Passwords became normal because they were simple to build, not because they were especially safe or convenient. Passkeys reverse that idea. The technology underneath is more advanced, but the action required from the user can be easier: unlock a trusted device and continue. If the industry can make recovery, portability, and cross-platform use as smooth as the sign-in itself, the password may finally stop being the default key to digital life.
