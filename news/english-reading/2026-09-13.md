Why the Password Is Finally Losing Its Job

For decades, the password has been one of the strangest parts of digital life. A person may use a powerful computer, a modern smartphone, and advanced artificial intelligence, yet still be asked to prove who they are by remembering a short secret. The system is familiar, but it has serious weaknesses. People forget passwords, reuse them across websites, and sometimes give them away to convincing fake login pages. Now a different method, called the passkey, is moving from a technical idea into everyday use.

A passkey changes the basic logic of signing in. With a password, the user and the website both depend on knowledge of the same secret. If an attacker steals that secret from the user or from a database, it can often be used elsewhere. A passkey instead uses public-key cryptography. When a passkey is created, the user's device keeps a private key, while the service receives a related public key. The private key does not need to be sent to the website during login.

The process feels simpler than the technology behind it. A user may visit a website, choose to sign in with a passkey, and then confirm the action with a fingerprint, face scan, or device PIN. The biometric data is not sent to the website. It remains on the device and is used locally to confirm that the person is allowed to use the credential. To the user, the experience can feel like unlocking a phone rather than entering a password.

This design solves one of the hardest problems in online security: phishing. Traditional phishing attacks often create a fake page that looks almost identical to a real login page. A victim types a username, password, and perhaps even a temporary security code. The attacker then uses those details on the real site. Passkeys are designed to resist this trick because the credential is connected to the legitimate website. A fake site cannot simply ask the user to reveal the private key.

The technology is based on standards developed through the FIDO Alliance and the World Wide Web Consortium. In August 2026, Web Authentication Level 3 became a full World Wide Web Consortium Recommendation. Web Authentication, together with related FIDO standards, provides the technical foundation that allows browsers, operating systems, devices, and websites to work with public-key credentials in a common way. This matters because a replacement for passwords is useful only if it works across a large part of the internet.

Adoption has moved quickly. According to the FIDO Alliance's 2026 global research, about five billion passkeys were in active use by May. In a survey of eleven thousand adults across ten countries, ninety percent said they were familiar with passkeys, while three quarters had enabled a passkey on at least one account. Among large organizations surveyed separately, more than two thirds were deploying, testing, or rolling out passkeys for employees.

Large technology companies are also changing their products around this idea. Microsoft reported in 2025 that nearly one million passkeys were being registered for Microsoft accounts each day. The company said passkey users completed sign-in much more successfully than password users and did so considerably faster. New Microsoft consumer accounts were also changed to be passwordless by default. The shift has continued into the workplace. Beginning in September 2026, Microsoft made passkeys the default authentication experience for certain users of its Entra identity platform who had previously relied on text messages or voice calls for authentication.

The move away from text-message codes is significant. For years, a password followed by a code sent to a phone was considered a useful form of multi-factor authentication. It is usually safer than a password alone, but it is not free from attack. Criminals can trick users into revealing codes, redirect phone numbers through SIM-related fraud, or create login pages that forward information in real time. Microsoft has announced plans to retire its own delivery of SMS and voice authentication for Entra ID in February 2027, while directing organizations toward phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys and security keys.

Passkeys also remove some ordinary frustrations. People no longer need to invent complicated strings, remember which version of a password they used, or reset a forgotten password as often. For companies, fewer password resets can mean fewer support requests. Security and convenience, which often seem to work against each other, can therefore improve at the same time.

Still, replacing a system used for decades is not simple. People use many devices, browsers, operating systems, and password managers. They replace phones, lose laptops, and move between technology ecosystems. A credential that is extremely secure but difficult to recover could create a new kind of problem. This is why modern passkey systems often allow credentials to be synchronized through trusted password managers or platform accounts. In September 2026, Google introduced a way for Android users to move passwords and passkeys directly between password managers without first exporting them into an unencrypted file. Such changes address an important concern: users should not have to remain with one provider simply because their digital credentials are difficult to move.

Organizations face additional complications. A company may have old applications, shared machines, special hardware, contractors, and employees who cannot all use the same authentication method. Research published through the FIDO Alliance in 2026 found strong interest in passkeys but also showed that large-scale deployment remained difficult for many organizations. Identity systems are often fragmented, so replacing passwords may require changes in technology, policy, recovery procedures, and employee training at the same time.

There is also a deeper lesson in the history of passwords. Their weakness was never simply that people were bad at creating them. Humans were being asked to perform a task that computers are better suited to handle: storing and proving possession of complex secrets. Advice such as making passwords longer and more unique improved security, but it placed more responsibility on the user. Password managers helped by moving much of that work back to software. Passkeys continue the same direction by removing the shared password itself from the normal login process.

Passwords will not disappear everywhere at once. Older systems will remain, recovery methods will still be necessary, and attackers will look for weaknesses around any new technology. Yet the center of online authentication is clearly changing. For much of the internet's history, signing in meant proving that you knew something. Increasingly, it means proving that you possess a trusted cryptographic credential and are authorized to use it. That small change in logic may finally allow the internet to move beyond one of its oldest and most persistent security problems.
