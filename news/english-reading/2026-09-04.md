What Does Open Source Really Mean for Artificial Intelligence?

When people call an artificial intelligence model “open source,” they may be describing very different things. One company may publish the model’s numerical weights, allowing anyone to download and run it. Another may also release the software used to train the model. A third may go further and publish the training data, research methods, and intermediate checkpoints created during development. All of these projects can appear open, yet they offer very different levels of transparency.

This distinction became especially visible in September 2026, when the Institute of Foundation Models in Abu Dhabi announced a family of artificial intelligence models called K2 Horizon. According to Reuters, the institute released not only model code and weights but also training data, development methods, and checkpoints. The models range from relatively compact systems to a much larger model with hundreds of billions of parameters. The project was presented as an attempt to make advanced AI research easier to inspect and reproduce.

To understand why this matters, it helps to know how modern AI models are built. A large language model does not begin as a finished program containing millions of carefully written rules. During training, it processes enormous amounts of data and gradually adjusts billions of numerical values known as parameters. The final collection of these values is often called the model weights. When developers publish those weights, other people can usually run the model without repeating the extremely expensive training process.

That is useful, but weights alone do not tell the whole story. Imagine receiving a finished cake without the recipe, the list of ingredients, or a description of the kitchen process. You can taste the cake and perhaps modify it, but reproducing exactly how it was made would be difficult. In a similar way, researchers who receive only model weights can study the finished system, yet they may not know precisely which data shaped it or which decisions were made during training.

Training data is particularly important because it influences what a model learns. If a dataset contains errors, cultural imbalances, duplicated material, or low-quality information, those weaknesses can affect the final model. Access to information about the dataset allows researchers to ask more detailed questions. Why does the model perform well in one language but poorly in another? Did it see material similar to a test during training? Are certain groups or regions poorly represented? Without enough information about the data, some of these questions are difficult to answer.

Checkpoints provide another layer of visibility. During training, developers usually save intermediate versions of a model. These snapshots show how the system changed over time. Researchers can compare them to study when particular abilities appeared, how mistakes developed, or how later stages of training affected behavior. Publishing such material is much less common than simply releasing the final weights because checkpoints can require large amounts of storage and may reveal details about the development process.

The debate over openness has grown because AI has become both more powerful and more expensive. Training a leading model can require large computing systems, specialized chips, engineering talent, and major financial investment. Companies therefore have strong reasons to protect their methods. Even when they release a model for public use, they may keep the training data or important parts of the process private. This can still provide real benefits to developers, but it makes independent reproduction harder.

There is also disagreement about the language used to describe these releases. In traditional software, open source has a relatively clear meaning: users can inspect, modify, and redistribute the source code under an appropriate license. AI is more complicated because the “source” of a trained model is not just ordinary computer code. The data, weights, training software, filtering rules, and development process can all affect the final result. As a result, researchers and policy groups have spent considerable effort trying to define what openness should mean for AI systems.

More transparency can improve scientific research. If researchers can examine the ingredients and process behind a model, they have a better chance of reproducing results and discovering why a system behaves in a certain way. Reproducibility is a basic principle of science: a result becomes more convincing when independent groups can test the same method and reach similar conclusions. Fully documented AI projects can also help universities and smaller research teams learn techniques that would otherwise remain inside large laboratories.

Open models can also support practical innovation. A company may want to adapt a model for a local language, a scientific field, or an internal business task. Running an openly available model on its own computers can offer more control over cost, privacy, and customization than relying entirely on a remote service. Developers can inspect the software, change parts of the system, and build tools that would be difficult to create around a completely closed model.

Openness, however, does not remove every barrier. Publishing the recipe for a huge model does not mean everyone can afford to cook it. Reproducing advanced training may still require enormous computing resources. Large datasets can be difficult to store and process. Researchers also need specialized knowledge to understand why particular choices were made. In this sense, information can be open while practical access remains unequal.

There are also questions about responsibility. Training datasets may contain copyrighted, private, or otherwise sensitive material, which can make complete publication difficult. Powerful models may also be adapted for uses their original developers did not intend. Supporters of greater openness argue that broad access allows more people to examine weaknesses and improve the technology. Critics worry that the same access can reduce a developer’s ability to control harmful applications. The balance between these goals is still being debated.

For ordinary users, the difference between open and closed AI may seem distant, but it can influence which products are available and who controls them. More open technology can allow smaller companies to compete, researchers to investigate claims, and organizations to run AI on their own infrastructure. Closed systems can offer simpler services and tighter control, but users must place more trust in the organizations operating them.

The important question, then, is not simply whether an AI model carries the label “open.” A better question is: open in what way? Can people run it, study it, modify it, understand its training data, and reproduce its development process? As AI becomes a basic part of software, science, and business, those differences will shape who can understand the technology rather than merely use it.
