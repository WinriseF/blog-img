The Invisible People Holding Up the Software World

When people imagine the technology industry, they often picture large companies, expensive data centers, and teams of well-paid engineers. Yet much of the digital world depends on a very different kind of worker: the open source maintainer. This person may be employed by a technology company, but just as often, they may work alone at night, answering bug reports and reviewing code for a project that thousands of businesses use without paying them directly.

Open source software is code that people are allowed to inspect, use, modify, and share under certain licenses. It is one of the foundations of modern computing. Web servers, programming languages, cloud platforms, mobile apps, artificial intelligence tools, and business systems all depend on open source components. A study known as Census II, organized by the Open Source Security Foundation and researchers connected with Harvard, examined more than half a million observations of open source libraries used in production applications across thousands of companies. The scale makes one point clear: open source is not a small side activity. It is part of the basic infrastructure of the software economy.

The strange part is that the economic system around it often does not match its importance. A company can build a profitable product using hundreds of free software packages while the people maintaining some of those packages receive little or no money. A 2024 survey of more than 500 open source maintainers, reported by Germany's Sovereign Tech Agency, found that about one third received no pay for their maintenance work. Another 30 percent earned some money, but not enough to make a living from it.

This creates a quiet risk. Maintaining software is not simply a matter of adding new features. Someone has to answer questions, review contributions, update documentation, remove old code, test new versions, study security reports, and decide which changes should be accepted. As a project becomes more successful, the amount of work can grow even when the number of maintainers does not.

The problem became especially visible in 2024 with XZ Utils, a set of compression tools widely used in Linux systems. Security researchers discovered that malicious code had been inserted into versions 5.6.0 and 5.6.1. The code was designed in a complicated way and could have created a path to unauthorized system access under certain conditions. Fortunately, the affected versions had not yet spread widely into stable Linux distributions, and the problem was discovered before the potential damage became much larger.

What made the incident important was not only the technical trick. It also showed how human pressure can become a security weakness. According to the Open Source Security Foundation and the OpenJS Foundation, the XZ case involved behavior that looked like a long social engineering effort. An unknown contributor gradually gained trust and influence around the project. After the incident, open source groups warned maintainers to be careful when unfamiliar people push aggressively for higher privileges, create false urgency, or pressure a tired maintainer to hand over control.

This is a difficult security problem because it cannot be solved only with better software scanners. If a project depends heavily on one exhausted person, an attacker may try to exploit the person's time, trust, or sense of responsibility. A maintainer who is doing unpaid work after a full working day may have less time to inspect every unusual change. In this sense, project health and cybersecurity are connected.

Governments and large technology organizations have started to treat the issue more seriously. The United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency has published an open source software security roadmap that includes goals such as understanding which open source projects are widely used, identifying high-risk dependencies, improving vulnerability response, and supporting security education for developers. The message is significant: software that is free to download can still be important enough to require public planning and investment.

Germany has gone further in experimenting with direct support. Its Sovereign Tech Fellowship pays people who maintain important open source infrastructure. After a pilot program in 2025 supported six maintainers working across 18 foundational technologies, the program expanded in 2026. The new group includes maintainers as well as community managers and technical writers. That broader definition matters because healthy software projects require more than code. Clear documentation, careful communication, and the ability to bring in new contributors can reduce dependence on a single expert.

Companies are also being pushed to think differently about responsibility. For years, many organizations treated open source packages like free raw material. If a library worked, they used it. If it broke, they often expected the community to fix it. A more sustainable model asks companies to understand what they depend on, support important projects financially, contribute engineering time, and avoid placing unreasonable demands on volunteers.

There is no simple funding model that works for every project. Some maintainers want employment. Others prefer donations, sponsorships, grants, consulting work, or public funding. A small library may need only a few hours of maintenance each month, while a widely used security tool may need a professional team. Too much corporate control can also create problems if it changes the goals of a community project or reduces its independence.

Still, the basic imbalance is becoming harder to ignore. Modern software has made it possible for millions of developers to build on work created by people they may never meet. That is one of open source's greatest strengths. It allows knowledge to spread quickly and prevents every programmer from rebuilding the same basic tools.

But shared code does not mean work without cost. Servers need maintenance, security reports need answers, and complicated projects need people who remember why important decisions were made years ago. When those people disappear, the problem is not only that a useful project may stop improving. Knowledge can disappear with them.

The future of open source may therefore depend less on finding a new technical invention than on recognizing an old economic fact: infrastructure requires maintenance. Roads, bridges, power systems, and public networks all need people whose work is often invisible when everything functions correctly. Software is beginning to look the same. The code may be free to copy, but keeping it reliable is still human work.
