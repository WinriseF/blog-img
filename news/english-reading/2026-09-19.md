Why Cities Are Learning to Fight Heat Before It Reaches the Air Conditioner

On a hot summer afternoon, two streets in the same city can feel surprisingly different. One may be lined with trees, shaded sidewalks and light-colored roofs. A few blocks away, another may be covered with dark asphalt, concrete walls and almost no shade. The air temperature may be similar on a weather app, yet the second street can feel much hotter. This difference is not just a matter of comfort. It is part of a larger problem known as the urban heat island effect.

Cities tend to be warmer than nearby rural areas because they are built from materials that absorb and store heat. Roads, roofs and walls receive solar energy during the day and release part of it later. At the same time, cities often have less vegetation and less exposed soil, so there is less shade and less water available for evaporation. Tall buildings can also reduce air movement in some places. The United States Environmental Protection Agency says urban areas in the country are commonly about one to seven degrees Fahrenheit warmer than surrounding areas during the day, with a smaller but still important difference at night.

This matters because heat is more than an uncomfortable weather condition. It puts stress on the human body and can worsen heart, kidney and respiratory problems. The World Health Organization warns that long periods of high daytime and nighttime temperatures can raise the risk of illness and death. Heat can also reduce work capacity, increase electricity demand and put pressure on power systems. Air conditioning helps protect people indoors, but relying on it alone creates another problem: the hotter a city becomes, the more electricity it needs for cooling.

That is why many researchers and city planners are trying to stop some of the heat before it reaches the air conditioner. One of the simplest tools is the tree.

Trees cool streets in two main ways. Their leaves block sunlight, keeping roads, walls and people in the shade. They also release water through a process called transpiration. As that water evaporates, it carries heat away from the surrounding environment. Research has repeatedly shown that shaded surfaces can be dramatically cooler than surfaces under direct sunlight. A large global review published in 2024 found that the cooling effect of urban trees can be substantial, although the result depends on the local climate, the shape of the city and the type of tree.

A major study published in Nature Communications in 2026 looked at 8,919 large urban areas around the world. The researchers estimated that existing urban tree cover removes roughly 41 to 49 percent of the air-temperature heat island effect that would occur without those trees. The average reduction in summer air temperature was modest when measured across entire urban populations, but the benefits were much larger in some places. The study also showed an important limitation: trees can reduce local urban heat, but they cannot cancel the broader warming caused by climate change.

The details become even more interesting when researchers compare different kinds of vegetation. A Nature Cities study published in 2025 examined how trees and lawns behaved during dozens of heat waves. Lawns increased evaporation quickly and produced strong short-term cooling, but they also used surface water rapidly. Trees provided steadier cooling because their roots could reach deeper water and their leaves could adjust how much water they released. The lesson is that simply adding more green space is not enough. A city must choose plants that can survive its climate and still provide useful cooling during the hottest periods.

Roofs offer another opportunity. A dark roof can become extremely hot in strong sunlight because it absorbs much of the incoming energy. A cool roof is designed to reflect more sunlight and release heat more efficiently. According to the Environmental Protection Agency, cool roofs can reduce the maximum indoor temperature of homes without air conditioning by about 1.2 to 3.3 degrees Celsius. That may sound small, but during a dangerous heat wave, a few degrees can make a meaningful difference.

Cities can also use lighter pavements, green roofs, shaded bus stops and better building design. These methods work through different physical processes, but the goal is similar: reduce the amount of solar energy that is absorbed near people, or move heat away before it builds up. In this sense, urban cooling is becoming less like a single technology and more like a system. Trees may cool a sidewalk, a reflective roof may reduce heat entering a building, and better street design may help air move through a neighborhood.

There are trade-offs. Trees need space, water and long-term maintenance. Their roots can conflict with underground pipes or sidewalks. Some species may struggle as temperatures rise. Green roofs can be expensive to install. Highly reflective surfaces must be chosen carefully because reflected sunlight can sometimes create glare or move heat toward nearby buildings. Cooling strategies that work well in a humid city may not be the best choice for a dry one.

There is also a social question. Tree cover and shade are not evenly distributed. Studies in several countries have found that lower-income neighborhoods often have fewer trees and higher exposure to heat. This means that urban cooling is not only an engineering problem. Decisions about where to plant trees, replace pavement or improve buildings can affect who receives the greatest protection from extreme heat.

The most useful shift may be a change in how cities think about cooling. For much of the modern era, the main response to heat was to cool the inside of buildings after the outdoor environment had already become hot. Now researchers are paying more attention to the surfaces, plants and street patterns that create heat in the first place.

Air conditioning will remain essential, especially during severe heat waves. But a cooler city does not have to begin with a colder room. It can begin with a tree over a sidewalk, a roof that reflects sunlight, or a street designed to store less heat. These measures do not eliminate the problem of rising temperatures, but they can reduce the amount of heat that people must fight every day.
