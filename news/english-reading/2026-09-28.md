Why Satellites Are Moving Closer to Earth

For much of the satellite age, one of the most valuable places in space was very far from Earth. A satellite placed about 36,000 kilometers above the equator can move around the planet at the same rate that Earth rotates. From the ground, it appears to remain almost fixed in the sky. This geostationary orbit made possible familiar services such as television broadcasting, weather observation, and long-distance communications.

Today, however, a different part of space is receiving much more attention. Companies and governments are launching large numbers of satellites into low Earth orbit, usually only a few hundred to a few thousand kilometers above the surface. The change is reshaping satellite internet, mobile communications, Earth observation, and even the way countries regulate radio spectrum.

The main attraction is simple: distance matters.

A radio signal traveling to a geostationary satellite must cross tens of thousands of kilometers before it can return to Earth. That journey creates noticeable delay. A low Earth orbit satellite is much closer, so signals can make the trip faster. The International Telecommunication Union, or ITU, says recent advances in low-orbit constellations have reduced latency and helped make satellite broadband more practical in places where building fiber or mobile towers is difficult.

Being closer also reduces the amount of signal power lost over distance. This can allow smaller user terminals and stronger links. For a person in a remote village, on a ship, or in an area damaged by a natural disaster, a satellite connection may reach places where ordinary ground infrastructure is too expensive, too slow to build, or temporarily unavailable.

But low orbit creates a new problem. A geostationary satellite can watch the same region continuously. A low-orbit satellite moves quickly across the sky. NASA notes that a satellite hundreds of kilometers above Earth can circle the planet in roughly an hour and a half. It soon disappears below the horizon from the viewpoint of one ground station.

To provide continuous service, operators therefore need many satellites working together. As one satellite moves away, another takes over. Ground stations must track this movement, networks must hand connections from one spacecraft to another, and radio frequencies must be carefully coordinated. The ITU describes modern low-orbit communication systems as combinations of satellites, user terminals, gateways, and other ground infrastructure rather than as simple machines floating alone in space.

This is why the word “constellation” has become so common. The satellites are designed as parts of a network. Some can also communicate directly with one another through inter-satellite links. A message may travel from one satellite to another before reaching a suitable gateway on the ground. This can extend coverage across oceans or regions with few ground stations.

The scale of the shift is remarkable. The ITU said in 2026 that roughly ten times more satellites are active than a decade earlier, with low Earth orbit constellations playing a major role in that growth. The European Space Agency’s 2026 Space Environment Report found that more than 300 launches took place in 2025 and more than 4,000 new payloads were placed into orbit. Large constellations are one reason a single rocket can now release many spacecraft at once.

More satellites can bring real benefits. Remote schools can gain broadband. Farmers can receive better communications and Earth-observation data. Ships and aircraft can remain connected far from cities. New direct-to-device systems also aim to let ordinary mobile phones communicate with satellites in situations where terrestrial networks are unavailable.

Yet the same growth creates a difficult question: how crowded can low Earth orbit become?

Space is enormous, but useful orbital regions are not unlimited. Satellites may share similar altitudes and cross one another’s paths at extremely high speeds. Old spacecraft, rocket stages, and fragments from previous collisions add to the danger. Even a small piece of debris can cause serious damage because objects in orbit move several kilometers every second.

The European Space Agency warns that some low-orbit regions are becoming increasingly congested. Its 2026 report says not enough satellites leave crowded orbits at the end of their missions, although compliance with disposal rules is improving. The agency also argues that removing old debris will be necessary if humanity wants to prevent collisions from creating still more debris.

The preferred solution is not to stop using low Earth orbit, but to manage it better. Modern satellites can often maneuver to avoid predicted collisions. Operators can share location data, design spacecraft to leave orbit after their missions, and lower them into the atmosphere, where they burn up. International guidelines have also moved toward shorter disposal periods. These steps reduce risk, but they depend on thousands of spacecraft and many different organizations behaving responsibly.

Radio interference is another challenge. Satellites need access to limited parts of the radio spectrum, and large moving constellations make coordination more complex. The ITU has developed new guidance for national regulators because satellite services can cross borders and interact with terrestrial mobile networks. Radio astronomers have also reported growing interference from satellite signals, including emissions that can make extremely weak signals from space harder to detect.

This shows why the move toward lower orbits is not simply a story about faster internet. It is a change in the architecture of space itself. The older model often relied on a small number of expensive satellites placed far away and designed to operate for many years. The new model can involve larger numbers of smaller spacecraft, frequent launches, software-controlled networks, and constant movement.

Geostationary satellites are not disappearing. Their ability to remain above one region is still valuable for broadcasting, weather monitoring, and other services. Medium Earth orbit also remains useful for navigation systems and communications. Different jobs favor different heights.

What is changing is the balance. Advances in rockets, satellite manufacturing, antennas, networking software, and computing have made large low-orbit systems more practical than they were in the past. At the same time, the demand for broadband and continuous connectivity has grown.

Moving satellites closer to Earth reduces one kind of distance, but it creates new forms of complexity. Networks must coordinate more spacecraft, regulators must manage more signals, and the space environment must absorb more traffic. The success of low Earth orbit will therefore depend on more than launching satellites cheaply. It will depend on whether humanity can turn a crowded moving system into a reliable and sustainable part of everyday infrastructure.
