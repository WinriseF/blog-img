Why Can a Computer Verify a Proof That Mathematicians Still Do Not Accept?

If you write down the prime numbers in order — 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 — they look almost random. Sometimes two primes sit close together, sometimes there is a long gap with none at all. Yet mathematicians have long suspected that this apparent randomness hides a precise pattern. In 1859 Bernhard Riemann connected the count of primes to a function now called the Riemann zeta function. The interesting zeros of that function, the inputs that make it equal zero apart from a few obvious cases, seemed to line up on a single vertical line where the real part equals one half. The Clay Mathematics Institute notes that this has been checked for the first 10,000,000,000,000 solutions, and still no general proof was accepted for more than a century and a half. That is the Riemann Hypothesis, and it is why a claim made this month has unsettled mathematics in a new way.

On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, released a four-page note saying that he had, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis and of its generalization to Dirichlet L-functions, a related family of functions used to study primes in arithmetic progressions. He did not release a conventional paper. He released code: a library written in Lean comprising about 17 million lines in 175,187 files. Lean is a proof assistant, software that turns mathematical statements and arguments into a form a computer can check step by step. Alpöge wrote that the work developed from early October to 16 November, that Claude far outpaced him near the finish line, and that he intended later to understand and write up the argument properly. For the moment, he said, he would present only the Lean formalization.

To see why that matters, it helps to picture what Lean actually checks. A human proof is a narrative that other humans read for gaps. A Lean proof is closer to a very long calculation whose every step must follow from explicit definitions and rules. Mathlib, the large shared library of formalized mathematics for Lean, already contains a vetted statement of the Riemann Hypothesis. If a new library proves a term of exactly that type, and if independent checker programs accept the exported proof using only the standard axioms, then the formal derivation is valid relative to those definitions. That is a strong kind of evidence, but it is evidence about a derivation inside a formal system. It does not by itself produce a human explanation of why the theorem is true.

On 21 November 2026 the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement certifying that both the statement and the proof are correct. Their rationale listed four checks: the statement is Mathlib's carefully vetted RiemannHypothesis; the library compiles without errors; two independent tools, comparator and con-leche in its compare mode, confirmed that the theorem proved is exactly Mathlib's statement; and several proof checkers, including Lean's official kernel and independent checkers in different languages, accepted the exported proof. Andrew Sutherland at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology published verification files showing that all 175,187 modules compiled, that the five headline theorems depend only on the three standard axioms, and that multiple kernels replayed the proof.

The dispute began almost immediately, and it is not mainly about whether the code compiles. In a preprint on PhilArchive, J. L. Nielsen of the Center for Topological Physics in Lawrence, Kansas, argued that the posted content does not contain a proof in the mathematical sense, even though it type-checks. Nielsen pointed to a predicate named anyGeometry defined as a function that returns True for every input. The name occurs 118 times in 24 files, including on the route to the main theorem. The geometric witness, Nielsen wrote, is a degenerate model in which volumes and norms are zero, curves collapse to points, and positivity conditions are defined as True. Most importantly, a height function that was supposed to be measured from an independent geometric object is instead overwritten with an explicit formula, hExpl6, after which the required equality holds by rfl, meaning by definition. In Nielsen's summary, the comparison becomes Riemann to Riemann: the same data supplies both sides, so the inequalities check a quantity against itself.

Verifiers who accept the formal result do not deny that such dummy definitions exist. Alpöge himself later called the Lean code full of garbage that still compiles. Their reply is about what a kernel verdict means. If the final theorem has exactly the type of Mathlib's RiemannHypothesis, and comparator tools confirm that match, then intermediate definitions, however ugly or vacuous, cannot by themselves make a checker accept a proof of the wrong statement. A vacuously true lemma inside a proof, in this view, makes the proof longer than it needs to be; it does not turn a correct formal derivation into an incorrect one. What the kernels decide is whether a term of the correct type was constructed from the allowed axioms. What they do not decide is whether the internal constructions correspond to the geometric objects a human reader expects them to name, or where the real mathematical ideas sit inside 17 million lines.

That distinction explains the present stalemate. Both sides agree the code checks. They disagree about what has been established. Nielsen grants that ordinary definitions suffice to type-check the term without extra axioms, but calls the defect structural: the geometric layer contributes nothing independent, so the argument a human would recognize as a proof is missing. Defenders of the verification grant that there is no human-readable proof yet and that the code needs cleaning and explanation, but they treat kernel acceptance plus statement matching as settling the formal question.

The Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the Riemann Hypothesis among its unsolved Millennium Prize Problems, and no prize has been awarded. That listing reflects process as much as judgment. Clay's rules require publication in a qualifying outlet, at least two years since publication, and general acceptance in the global mathematics community before a proposed solution is considered. A Lean archive posted on a personal website in November 2026 meets none of those conditions yet, whatever its formal status. Meanwhile mathematicians quoted in news reports describe the proof as not yet understood, drawing on decades of number theory in ways no single reader can yet survey. Alpöge's own note is candid on this point: he says he is presenting a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics, and hopes to simplify and explain it later.

What has changed, then, is the location of trust. For a 167-year-old problem, the strongest public evidence is no longer a manuscript that referees can read in months, but a machine-checked artifact that referees can replay in days and that almost no one can yet narrate. That is a real advance in one sense: a false formal derivation of the wrong statement would have been caught by the statement-matching and kernel checks that the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative describe. What remains unresolved is the older mathematical demand that a proof should also teach: which definitions are doing honest work, which are placeholders, and how the tens of intertwined arguments Alpöge mentions can be distilled into ideas other mathematicians can test, reuse and accept. Until that human account exists and survives scrutiny, the Riemann Hypothesis will occupy an unfamiliar position — formally verified in Lean, still listed as unsolved by the Clay Mathematics Institute, and not yet settled in the sense mathematics has traditionally required.
