Why Computer Chips Are Breaking Themselves Into Pieces

For most of the history of modern computing, a powerful processor was imagined as one large piece of silicon. Engineers tried to place more and more functions onto that single die: processor cores, memory controllers, graphics units, and other circuits. The basic idea was simple. If everything was close together, communication could be fast and efficient.

That approach worked extremely well for decades. But as chips became larger and more complex, a problem appeared. Making one enormous piece of perfect silicon is difficult and expensive. A tiny manufacturing defect can damage part of a chip, and the larger the chip is, the greater the chance that a defect will appear somewhere on it. At the same time, the newest manufacturing processes have become extremely costly. These pressures are changing one of the most basic ideas in computer design.

Instead of building one giant chip, many companies are now building processors from several smaller pieces called chiplets. Each chiplet can perform a different job. One may contain central processing unit cores, another may handle input and output, and another may provide graphics or artificial intelligence functions. These pieces are then connected inside the same package so that, from the computer's point of view, they behave much like one processor.

The idea is partly about manufacturing economics. Imagine trying to make a large glass window with no scratches. The larger the window becomes, the harder it is to produce a perfect one. Silicon manufacturing has a similar problem. Smaller dies are statistically more likely to come out of a wafer without serious defects. An IEEE overview of chiplet technology explains that smaller dies can achieve higher manufacturing yield, meaning that a larger percentage of the pieces made on a wafer can actually be used.

This is one reason AMD became an important early supporter of the chiplet approach in high-performance processors. Rather than continuing to make larger monolithic chips, the company separated major functions into smaller building blocks. AMD says this strategy allowed it to scale its Zen-based processors by adding more chiplets while also separating the development of computing cores from input and output functions. The approach later spread across Ryzen desktop processors, EPYC server chips, and other high-performance products.

Chiplets also make it possible to use different manufacturing processes for different parts of a processor. This matters because not every circuit benefits equally from the newest and most expensive technology. High-performance computing cores may need an advanced process with extremely small transistors. Input and output circuits, however, may work perfectly well on an older and cheaper process. With a monolithic design, all these parts are usually manufactured together. With chiplets, engineers can choose the process that makes the most sense for each part.

Intel has moved in a similar direction. Its Core Ultra processors, originally developed under the code name Meteor Lake, became the company's first client processor design built from multiple tiles using its Foveros packaging technology. The package combines specialized pieces rather than treating the processor as one traditional die. This shows that chiplets are not limited to giant data-center processors. The same design idea has reached ordinary laptops.

But breaking a chip into pieces creates a new problem: the pieces must communicate extremely quickly. A traditional monolithic processor has microscopic connections inside one piece of silicon. Chiplets have to send information across boundaries between separate dies. If those connections are too slow or consume too much energy, the advantages of splitting the processor can disappear.

This is why advanced packaging has become almost as important as transistor technology. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, or TSMC, has developed packaging systems such as CoWoS that place logic chiplets and high-bandwidth memory close together using dense interconnections. Intel has developed technologies including Foveros and embedded bridges for similar purposes. Modern packaging is no longer just a protective shell around a finished chip. It is becoming part of the computer's architecture.

The industry is also trying to make chiplets more standardized. The Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express, known as UCIe, defines an open way for dies to communicate inside a package. The consortium behind it includes companies such as AMD, Arm, Intel, Nvidia, Qualcomm, Samsung, TSMC, Google Cloud, Meta, and Microsoft. Its goal is ambitious: chiplets could eventually become more like reusable components rather than pieces designed only for one product.

That idea resembles the history of personal computers. A desktop computer can combine a processor from one company, memory from another, storage from another, and many other components because the interfaces between them are standardized. Chiplets could bring a similar form of modularity inside the processor package itself. The UCIe 3.0 specification, released in 2025, increased supported data rates to as much as 64 gigatransfers per second and added features for more flexible and manageable multi-chip systems.

A true mix-and-match market for chiplets is still difficult, however. Connecting dies from different companies involves more than agreeing on an electrical interface. Designers must consider heat, power delivery, physical size, testing, software support, and reliability. A chiplet that works well in one package may not automatically fit another. Advanced packaging also adds cost and manufacturing complexity. For smaller or simpler products, a single monolithic chip can still be the better solution.

There is another limit: splitting a processor too aggressively can increase communication delays. Some operations depend on data moving rapidly between different parts of a chip. Engineers must therefore decide which functions should stay close together and which can be separated. Chiplet design is not simply a matter of cutting a large chip into smaller squares. It requires a new way of thinking about where computation should happen and how data should move.

The change matters because it shows how the semiconductor industry is responding to the slowing of an old pattern. For decades, progress was strongly associated with making transistors smaller and placing more of them on one chip. That process continues, but it is no longer the only path to better computers. Engineers are now improving systems by combining different kinds of silicon, packaging them more closely, and building faster connections between them.

The processor of the future may therefore look less like one perfect piece of silicon and more like a carefully planned city. Different districts will perform different jobs. Roads between them will have to carry enormous amounts of traffic. Some areas will use the newest technology, while others will use older but cheaper methods. The challenge is not simply to make every part smaller. It is to make all the parts work together as one efficient system.
