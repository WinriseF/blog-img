Why Software Is Getting an Ingredient List

When a serious security problem appears in a popular software library, the first question inside many companies is surprisingly basic: “Are we using it?” The answer is not always easy to find. Modern software is rarely built from scratch. A single application may depend on hundreds or even thousands of outside components, many of which depend on other components that the original developer never selected directly.

This hidden complexity is one reason the technology industry is paying more attention to the Software Bill of Materials, usually called an SBOM. The idea is similar to the ingredient list on a food package. Instead of telling you what is inside a meal, an SBOM records the components inside a piece of software, including names, versions, suppliers, and relationships between dependencies.

The need became especially clear after the Log4Shell crisis in late 2021. Log4Shell was a severe vulnerability in Log4j, a widely used open-source Java logging library. The library was often buried deep inside other products as a dependency of another dependency. Organizations suddenly had to discover which systems contained vulnerable versions. Security agencies later noted that companies without good software inventories often had to perform slow manual searches, while organizations with better component records could respond more directly.

The problem was not that companies had no idea what software they owned. Many already had lists of applications and servers. The difficulty was knowing what was inside those applications. A company might know that it uses a certain business platform, but not that the platform includes a particular open-source library several layers below the surface.

An SBOM tries to make those hidden layers visible.

The concept is not new, but governments and industry groups have made it more formal in recent years. The U.S. National Telecommunications and Information Administration described an SBOM as a formal record of software components and their supply-chain relationships. Its minimum elements include information such as the component name, supplier, version, unique identifiers, dependency relationships, the author of the SBOM data, and a timestamp.

More recent guidance from the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, or CISA, continues to treat SBOMs as a foundation for software transparency. In 2025, CISA published updated minimum elements and also joined international partners in promoting a shared vision for SBOM use. The goal is not simply to create more documents. It is to make software inventories usable by machines, so security tools can compare them with vulnerability databases and other risk information automatically.

This matters because software changes constantly. A component that looks safe today may receive a serious vulnerability report next month. Without a detailed inventory, a security team may need to scan systems, contact vendors, inspect code, and wait for answers. With a reliable SBOM, the organization can search its component records and quickly narrow the list of products that may be affected.

That does not mean an SBOM automatically tells a company that it is in danger. A vulnerable library may be present but never used in the risky way. Some products include code that is disabled or unreachable. This is why another format, called Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange, or VEX, is sometimes used with SBOM data. VEX helps a supplier explain whether a known vulnerability actually affects a product and why.

Together, these tools can reduce wasted effort. Imagine a company using fifty products that all contain the same library. A vulnerability is announced, but only five products use the dangerous function. An SBOM can help identify all fifty products. VEX information can help the security team focus first on the five that are truly exposed.

The idea also changes the relationship between software buyers and software makers. In the past, customers often received a finished product with little knowledge of its internal dependencies. SBOMs push the industry toward greater transparency. A hospital, bank, factory, or government agency can ask a supplier what components are inside a product before or after buying it.

For developers, this creates both benefits and extra work. Development teams already use package managers and build tools that know much of this information. In many cases, SBOM generation can be added to automated build pipelines. Common machine-readable formats include SPDX and CycloneDX. This means an SBOM can be created whenever a release is built, rather than written manually.

However, producing an SBOM is easier than maintaining a useful SBOM program. The data must be accurate, current, complete enough for the intended purpose, and connected to other security processes. A list that is created once and then forgotten can become outdated quickly. An organization also needs a way to store, search, protect, and update the information.

There is another challenge: an SBOM can reveal details about how software is built. Some suppliers worry that giving too much information to everyone could help attackers study a product. This is why access control and distribution rules matter. Transparency does not necessarily mean publishing every SBOM openly on the internet. It can mean sharing the right information with customers, regulators, or security teams under controlled conditions.

SBOMs also do not replace secure development. Knowing that a vulnerable component exists is useful, but the organization still has to patch it, replace it, reduce exposure, or accept the risk. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology treats software inventory and supply-chain visibility as parts of a broader secure development process, not as a complete solution by themselves.

This distinction is important because cybersecurity often fails through weak processes rather than a lack of data. A company can own a perfect ingredient list and still respond slowly. It can also generate large numbers of security alerts without knowing which systems matter most. SBOMs become valuable only when they are connected to decisions.

Their larger significance is that modern software has become too interconnected to manage as a black box. Applications are built from layers of commercial code, open-source projects, cloud services, operating-system libraries, and development tools. A weakness in one small component can travel far beyond the team that originally wrote it.

For years, software benefited from this ability to reuse code without requiring everyone to examine every ingredient. That reuse made development faster and cheaper. The security challenge is learning how to keep the benefits of shared components while understanding the risks they carry.

An SBOM does not make software safe. It makes software more visible. In a world where one small library can appear inside thousands of products, visibility is becoming a form of defense.
